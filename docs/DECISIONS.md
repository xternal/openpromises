## How the decisions are applied

Notes made while building, so a reader can see how each decision was read. None of them changes a decision; anything that would is proposed below and waits for the owner.

- **Decision 9 and this repository.** The engine is built in its own repository from 9 October 2026, as the owner asked when he started it. This repository only reads the three sites and never changes them; each site moves in its own repository. Public Ledger moved first, on 12 October 2026 (decision 21; xternal/public-ledger#81–#83); the others not before 29 October.
- **Decision 4 and private declarations.** The editors list is a file the configuration points to (`editorial.editorsFile`, default `editors.yaml`), and `openpromises validate --editors <path>` can read it from somewhere else. A site that has promised its editors to keep their declarations private can therefore keep the list out of its public repository and give it to its checks privately; a site that publishes its list keeps it beside the cards.
- **Corrections and reviews are not optional.** RFC §7 lists `corrections` and `reviews` among Public Ledger's modules. Because principles 3 (public corrections) and 4 (two editors, recorded in the card) are rules for every site, both are always on. The names are still accepted in `modules`, so the RFC's example configuration works unchanged.

- **Deadline markers and date order.** Events are in date order except automatic ones (the RFC's rule) and `deadline` markers, which Public Ledger dates in the future. Without this exception, the first event added after a future deadline marker would break the order.
- **The YAML the engine writes** quotes dates and the `on` key of a review, so that YAML 1.1 readers such as PyYAML read them as text, not as dates and `true`.

## Accepted on 9 October 2026, after the Public Ledger dry run

Both came from the Public Ledger dry run of 9 October 2026 (`pnpm dry-run`, [tools/dry-run](../tools/dry-run/README.md)), and Pavel Guzhikov accepted both as recommended the same evening. [UPGRADING.md](UPGRADING.md) is decision 13 for a site's owner; FORMAT.md §5 and §6 carry decision 14.

**13. Upgrading: what never breaks, and how a site upgrades.** Public Ledger changed its card format on 9 October 2026, two hours after release 0.1.0, and 0.1.0 then refused 29 of its 54 cards. Sites and the engine will both keep changing, so a site needs to know what an upgrade can and cannot do to it. Decided:

1. **Cards.** A card valid in format v1 stays valid in every later release. The format only grows, by optional fields; whether a site requires one is the site's own setting, never an upgrade's. A field added to the format may be filled in once on a published entry without a correction (FORMAT.md keeps the list, with the date each field was added), as Public Ledger's `LATE_FIELDS` allows. If a v2 is ever needed, the engine reads v1 cards by converting them on read, as it reads the sites' older formats now, so no site rewrites a published card.
2. **Checks.** A new or stricter rule arrives as a warning that says from which version it becomes an error, and becomes one no sooner than the next minor release. An upgrade never stops a site publishing without notice.
3. **What the public sees.** Feed entry ids, page addresses, structured-data ids and open-data columns never change for an existing card; tests hold them still. Changed wording is listed in the changelog, and a site that wants its own words sets them in `messages`.
4. **Code.** The six packages share one version and are released together. A patch only fixes. A minor adds; while the version is 0.x it may also break, but only with steps under "Upgrading" in the changelog. Version 1.0 comes once Public Ledger and Borough Book run on the engine; after that, breaking changes come only in a major, and the old way keeps working, with a warning, for one major more. Node 22 is supported until its end of life (30 April 2027).
5. **Proof before every release.** The fixtures' outputs of each released version are kept (`fixtures/compat/<version>/`: check results, feed ids, addresses, open data) and every later version must reproduce them or name the change as intended. A report of the packages' exported names and types fails CI when one disappears. The dry run for each live site must find nothing worse (Pavel runs Russia Ledger's privately).
6. **How a site upgrades.** A dependency bot opens one pull request that moves every `@openpromises/*` package to the new version; the site's CI runs `openpromises validate` and its build; the pull request links the changelog's Upgrading notes; it is merged when green. A renamed configuration key is rewritten by `openpromises upgrade`, which touches the configuration and never a card.

Why: sites can upgrade without fear and without reading code, and the engine can still improve. The cost is discipline at release time (the compatibility fixtures, the API report and the dry runs), which is mostly automatic.

**14. Who made a cost, who must deliver, and who brought it about, in format v1.** Public Ledger added three facts on 9 October 2026 that format v1 has no place for, and the AI reporter's posting rules (its RFC, Amendment 1) read them on both Public Ledger and Borough Book. Decided, as optional fields (decision 13.1):

- `cost.by`: who made a cost's central figure, `{ kind: official | party | independent, name }`. A late field. A site requires it with `money.costedBy: "required"`.
- `responsible`: the body that would have to act to deliver the promise as worded, as of now, named by its role (an actor whose kind is in `actors.responsible`, such as `government`), or `null` when no body in power is committed. A card-level fact, kept current by ordinary edits. A site requires it with `actors.responsibleRequired: true`. It is not called `outcome_by`, because Public Ledger's `outcome_by` meant something else until 9 October 2026, and a reader of older data would misread it.
- `brought_about_by`: who brought the outcome about when it was not the card's own actor; only on a card whose status is in progress or finished.

The checks Public Ledger makes on them come with them: the actor exists, `responsible` names a body and never a party or a person, `cost.by` goes with a cost and only with one. Public Ledger's converter maps `costed_by`, `outcome_by` and `brought_about_by` onto these. Why: one reporter, one standard. The alternative, keeping them in each site's `x`, would let each site name them differently and leave the engine unable to check them.

## Accepted on 9 October 2026, from Public Ledger's migration plan

Raised on 9 October 2026 by Public Ledger's migration plan (draft pull request xternal/public-ledger#68, `docs/OPENPROMISES_MIGRATION.md`), and accepted by Pavel Guzhikov as recommended the same night.

Four of the plan's questions were already settled that evening, by decisions 13 and 14, and released in 0.2.0. Who made a cost is `cost.by` (the plan's `costed_by`, converted with its correction paths). Late fields exist, with `cost.by` the first. Who must deliver is `responsible`, limited to configured kinds of actor (the plan's `outcome_by`). Cost changes are feed entries, and a site hands in its own contract and figures entries (`publishFiles({ site })`). COMPARISON.md describes the new meaning of `outcome_by` since 0.2.0.

**15. A converted site may fill in, once, the fields its old format had no place for.** Public Ledger's published costs have no quality label, because its format had no field for one. Adding the 29 labels after conversion would otherwise need 29 corrections for mistakes nobody made. Decided: each converter lists the v1 fields its format lacked (Public Ledger: `versions[n].parameters.cost.quality`). For a site with that `legacy` format, those fields join the late fields: they may be filled in once, then change only by correction. The list lives in the engine, per format, never in a site's configuration, so no site can make its own history editable. Editors add the labels, starting from what the site already shows; the converter invents none. Cost: a small change to the append-only check, and a list per converter.

**16. Notes on linked contracts and on actors become fields, so migration loses none.** Public Ledger's files hold 63 comment lines: why each contract is linked to its card, and where an actor's role or official pages were checked, with links and dates. They are editorial reasoning and provenance, which readers should see and checks should keep, not comments that vanish when a file is rewritten. Decided: three optional fields. `links.contracts[].note` holds why the contract is linked. An actor role's `source` is `{ url, quote? }`, showing where the role is confirmed. `same_as_checked_on` is the date the official pages were checked. `openpromises migrate` moves each comment into its field, and lists any comment it cannot place, so nothing is dropped silently. The other route keeps YAML comments by editing files in place: cheaper, but the notes stay invisible to readers and to every tool. Cost: three optional fields, and a migrate step that reads comments.

**17. Public Ledger keeps its own draft format until the intake package.** The engine reads `drafts/` one level deep, and a v1 draft needs `actor_id` and `area`. Public Ledger's intake drafts sit in dated folders and may lack both. Decided: Public Ledger's intake keeps writing its own drafts, and only published cards move to v1. Drafts follow when the intake moves (RFC E4). Cost: none now; two draft formats live side by side until E4.

**18. `standing` is required for parties, not for everyone.** FORMAT.md says an actor needs `standing` when the site sets `standing: manual`, but the engine never checks it. On Public Ledger, ten of 19 actors have none: eight people, who take their party's, and two governments. Decided: FORMAT.md says what is meant, that with `standing: manual` every party states its standing and a person takes their party's. A check for parties arrives as a warning in 0.3.0 and becomes an error in 0.4.0 (decision 13.2). Public Ledger's parties all pass it already. Cost: a sentence and a small check.

**19. Public Ledger moves after 17 November 2026.** Decision 9 says nothing moves before 29 October. Public Ledger's own plan now moves it after its 30-day build series, ending 17 November. Decided: keep decision 9 as the earliest date, and record 17 November as Public Ledger's planned start. Cost: none; Borough Book's move (E3) follows later.

## Accepted on 12 October 2026

**21. Public Ledger switches now: from 12 October 2026, done by 21 October.** Replaces decision 20 (not before 1 December) and, for Public Ledger, decision 9's start on 29 October. What decision 20 waited for is already done: every published card has two editors' approvals (xternal/public-ledger#77, #78), Public Ledger runs 0.4.0 (#80) and its report-only check lists only the 29 cost quality labels, and the dry run is good or better. Done by 21 October leaves a quiet week before the Budget-day launch on 28 October to fix anything the switch brings up, and the AI Journalist's first public weeks from 17 November then rest on settled data, which was decision 20's aim. The switch goes in its own pull requests: a converter back to Public Ledger's shape with round-trip tests; `openpromises migrate`, checked by `openpromises validate --base`, the site's parity check and screenshots; then the quality labels and the blocking check. Decided by Pavel Guzhikov on 12 October 2026 (about 13:10 BST, in Public Ledger's session) and confirmed here at 13:47 BST, with the 21 October date.

**22. A site may publish a card once its checks pass; a reader's submission still waits for two editors.** A new setting, `editorial.publish`. `"approved"`, the default, keeps RFC principle 4: every published card has the editors' approvals. `"checked"` publishes a card once every automatic check passes: its words match their source word for word (a stored-source span or an exact archive check: an editor's check by eye counts only on a card the editors approve), every event that moves the status has its evidence, the cost has its source, and the format and history checks pass. Editors' approvals then stop being a gate and become a label on the card (decision 23). A card whose origin is `reader_submission`, or that does not say its origin, still needs the approvals before it is published: anyone can submit one, so that is where false or defamatory material would come from. For a site that sets `"checked"`, this amends RFC principles 4 and 7: only editors approve and machines never do, but a card drafted by a machine from an official source can be published by its checks. Public Ledger sets it; Borough Book and Russia Ledger keep the default unless they choose otherwise. Decided by Pavel Guzhikov in Public Ledger's session on 12 October 2026 and confirmed here at 14:19 BST, as a setting for each site. Why each site chooses: an engine upgrade never quietly loosens a site's rules (decision 13), and Russia Ledger is where an unchecked card could put people at risk.

**23. Every card shows how far it has been verified, worked out once by the engine.** One function, `verification`, reads a card's reviews with the approvals rule's tests (on the editors list, within their dates, never about their own party) and gives one of four levels. Every output uses it: the card component, the open data (JSON and CSV), the feeds and the Markdown. The words are the site's, through messages; the defaults are Pavel Guzhikov's:
- no review: "Not yet verified by an editor";
- automated reviews only: "Checked automatically by {reviewer}, not yet verified by an editor", with the reviewer's name as readers see it (`editorial.renamed`);
- one editor: "Verified by {editors}", such as "Verified by Pavel Guzhikov" or "Verified by 1 independent editor";
- two editors or more: the same, such as "Verified by Pavel Guzhikov and 1 independent editor".

"Verified" is kept for people. Each editor chooses whether to be named or counted, with a new field in the editors list, `named: true`; an editor without it is counted, not named, so privacy is the default. This replaces Public Ledger's own list of named editors. Decided and confirmed with decision 22.

**24. How a feature reaches the sites.** What belongs in the engine, and the four steps from a decision to a site enforcing it, are in [CONTRIBUTING.md](../CONTRIBUTING.md#how-a-feature-reaches-the-sites), for every session to follow. In short: the format, checks, publishing rules, labels, feed and API shapes, reusable components and intake go in the engine, and so does anything two sites need; a site's own features stay in the site. A site never edits the engine's code; it asks for the change. Decided and confirmed with decision 22.

**25. Products are called by their domain.** LedgerGov.UK (earlier entries say Public Ledger), BoroughBook.UK (Borough Book) and RussiaLedger.com (Russia Ledger, or the bilingual tracker). Decided by Pavel Guzhikov on 12 October 2026: a product is easier to name by its domain, where it has one. The documentation and comments use them from now on. Earlier entries here and released versions in CHANGELOG.md keep the names they were written with, as history. Names in code stay as they are, since sites use them: `fromPublicLedger`, `fromBoroughBook`, `legacy: "public-ledger"`, `fixtures/public-ledger`, `PUBLIC_LEDGER_DIR`. So do the fixtures, which are copies of each site's data, site name included.

**26. The engine never holds or asks for an AI key.** No package of OpenPromises contains, reads or requires a key for an AI model, or for any other paid service. Today none of them calls a model at all, and the engine makes no network calls (principle 8). When the intake or reviewer package is built (RFC §10, E4), it takes a model the site passes in, with the site's own provider and key, and works with none. A site that uses a model brings its own key, and nothing in this repository, its CI or its releases ever uses the owner's. Decided by Pavel Guzhikov at 20:05 BST on 12 October 2026: he does not want his key used for any setup of the SDK. His own sites each keep their own key in their own repository, which is theirs to manage.

## Accepted on 18 October 2026

**28. What a promise costs, and what that cost compares with: RFC-0002, as recommended.** Pavel Guzhikov asked on 17 October 2026 for "an educated calculation of a cost of a promise", in money, people, time and changes, with comparisons. [RFC-0002](RFC-0002.md) puts it in the engine, for all three sites. Its nine questions (§9) are decided as recommended:
- Q1: one fixed basket per site, the same units in the same order on every card, never chosen card by card.
- Q2: neutral words, "Equal to the cost of …" and "Столько же стоит: …", never "could build", "instead", "можно было бы" or "вместо". Pavel's own example wording is not used.
- Q3: the site may publish its own estimate (`cost.by.kind: editorial`, `quality: modelled`, a required method, at least ±25%), labelled "Our estimate", only when no official body, party or independent source has costed the promise.
- Q4: the first release has money, comparisons and people. Time and the delivery path are optional fields. The reference class is shown once there are at least 10 comparable cards.
- Q5: the basket lives in the site's configuration, with a source for each unit.
- Q6: comparisons are in the open data, not in the feeds.
- Q7: a basket has at least 3 units. A card shows 3, and a table lists them all.
- Q8: each price year is shown, and prices are adjusted only with a deflator the site supplies and names.
- Q9: delivery-path step types are listed in the configuration, with labels, like the status ladder.

The baskets in RFC-0002 Appendix A stay candidates: each site fixes its own from them, in its own pull request, before any card shows a comparison. Accepted by Pavel Guzhikov on 18 October 2026 at about 19:35 BST ("Q1–Q9 as recommended"). Why: it gives readers a sense of scale with one standard for every party, and keeps the numbers' sources in view.

## Accepted on 19 October 2026

**29. Every promise can tell its whole story on one page.** Pavel Guzhikov asked for a "Promise timeline" for each promise: from the original words to everything found later, words, actions and solutions, as a page people share. Most of it was already on the card, in separate sections: the versions, the timeline, the replies, the corrections and the cost changes. The engine now puts them in one dated line: `cardStory()` in `@openpromises/publish`, `storyMarkdown()` and a `PromiseStory` component, made to stand on its own page. It is built only from what the card records, so it adds nothing an editor has not checked: an action is there only as an event with its evidence, and a delivery step only when it is met by one or still expected. The same layout and order for every party, and neutral words, as for every label (principle 5). Not part of this decision: a share image per promise, which needs an image renderer the engine does not have, and finding later words and actions automatically, which is each site's intake and needs the AI Journalist's own evaluation first. Decided by Pavel Guzhikov on 19 October 2026 ("story as recommended").

**30. Promises close to the way in, the same way on every site.** Pavel Guzhikov asked for promises to stand out nearer the entrance of each site, so readers see they are worth opening: one UX approach for all. On 19 October 2026 the three home pages showed them fifth (LedgerGov.UK), sixth (BoroughBook.UK) or not at all (RussiaLedger.com), and none used the engine's list, so each had drifted. The engine gains `PromiseSpotlight` and `spotlight()`: how many promises and where they stand, the three latest changes and the three open promises due next, each a link straight to its card with "Open the promise →", then a link to them all; every `PromiseList` row says "Open the promise →" too. The cards are chosen by rule, never by hand: a hand-picked list on the most visible spot of a site would read as the site's own view (principle 5). Each site places it second, after its own opening, not in place of it, since the budget statement stays the main product of LedgerGov.UK and RussiaLedger.com; adds a line to its opening ("N promises tracked →"); opens a card in one click; and keeps "Promises" in its menu. Each does this in its own pull request, and RussiaLedger.com compares visits to promise pages for a week before and after with its own page counter. Decided by Pavel Guzhikov on 19 October 2026 ("UX как рекомендовано").

**31. An editor's note on a review may be in one of the site's languages.** Every text a site writes is in all its languages, but a review's note is the editor's own record of what they checked, written once and never edited (reviews are history). RussiaLedger.com's editors write theirs in Russian, and 188 of its review notes failed the engine's check for want of English that could only be added by rewriting history. The note now needs at least one of the site's languages, and no language the site does not have; readers of another language see it as written. Everything else a site writes still needs every language. Decided by Pavel Guzhikov on 19 October 2026, as part of «реши вопрос с RussiaLedger и полностью обнови все что надо».

**32. Sites take each release as soon as it is out; releases come in batches.** Four releases in two days (0.7.0 to 0.10.0) left the sites on 0.4.0 and 0.6.0: each waited pnpm's one-day minimum age, a site upgrade was put off to the next day, and by then the next release had come. The wait guards against a hijacked package, but OpenPromises' own packages already have stronger guards: they are published only by this repository's Release workflow, with provenance, after the owner approves each one. Decided:
- A site excludes `@openpromises/*`, and nothing else, from pnpm's minimum release age, and installs each release as soon as it is published, in its own pull request.
- In exchange, its CI runs `npm audit signatures` and checks each `@openpromises/*` package's provenance: built by `.github/workflows/release.yml` in github.com/xternal/openpromises from the tag of its own version, covering the tarball the registry serves.
- When a release is published, each site's upgrade pull request opens at once, by the coordinating session or by a dependency bot grouped on `@openpromises/*`; no "a day later" reminders.
- Releases come when `main` holds changes the sites should take together, not after every merge; a fix that cannot wait goes out on its own.

This replaces the "no exclusions" rule of CONTRIBUTING.md step 3 and UPGRADING.md. Decided by Pavel Guzhikov on 19 October 2026 («решение 32 как предложено»), after he called the release-then-wait cycle «как-то ТУПО».

## Accepted on 20 October 2026

**33. Every area, empty ones too.** Pavel Guzhikov's complaint about LedgerGov.UK's page for the Prime Minister: it shows what he promised on the price of a pint and VAT, but not whether he has promised anything on energy, AI or the long-term future. An actor's page listed only the areas their cards are in, so an area with nothing showed no sign of it, and a reader could not tell "promised nothing" from "not looked at". "None recorded" says what the site holds, not what was said: the first use, on 20 October 2026, found energy and AI promises the Prime Minister had made that LedgerGov.UK had not yet recorded, so a site that shows the table keeps its intake current in the areas it shows empty. Each site places it on its actor pages in its own pull request. Decided by Pavel Guzhikov on 20 October 2026 ("follow your recommendations for … Andy Burnham as a separate page on our ledgergov.uk").

**34. An actor's page tells their promises as one story: a timeline, the key promises and the promises made each month.** Pavel Guzhikov asked on 20 October 2026 for LedgerGov.UK's actor pages to feel like a "special project": an official photo, the key promises and what was delivered, and a timeline of how the promises progress. - `actorTimeline()` and `ActorTimeline`: everything dated on the actor's published promises, grouped by day, each entry linking to its cards: when each was promised and where, rewordings and restatements, every step on the ladder and every ending, replies, and the deadline of each open promise still to come. **35. An editor's check of a quote may be recorded after publishing, and every card says what was checked.** On 20 October 2026 USLedger.org published its first twenty cards, the twenty promises of the 2024 Republican Platform. Each quote was matched character for character in a stored copy of the platform (a `source_text` span), which the rules accept for publishing. That evening Pavel Guzhikov checked all twenty against the official PDF himself, but the cards could not record it: `quote_checked_on` was history once a card was published, and `openpromises review --quote-checked` refused and pointed to a correction. A correction fixes our own mistake (FORMAT.md §9); an editor's check made after publishing adds a fact and rewrites none, which is what a late field is for. Meanwhile every one of those pages said "Quote not yet checked against its source", although a machine had found the words in the stored copy. Decided:
- `versions[n].quote_checked_on` is a late field: it may be filled in once on a published version that has none, with no correction. Once it has a day, changing it is a correction like any other.
- `openpromises review --quote-checked` records it on a published card too. From an editor who has already approved the card, it records only the check, never a second approval. `--on` gives the day of a check made earlier: never in the future, and never before the version was recorded.
- Every card says the strongest check that holds: an editor's at the source, with its day; else a machine's word-for-word match in a stored copy of the source (a stored-source span or an exact archive check); else "not yet checked". The same words for every card and every party.

Decided by Pavel Guzhikov on 20 October 2026 ("5 fix if something useful needed, yes").

## Accepted on 22 October 2026

**36. A promise is read against the forecast made at the time, and a card can say when circumstances changed.** On 22 October 2026 Pavel Guzhikov brought a critique from a public discussion: a promise tracker punishes a government that promised 3% inflation and got 5%, though without its measures it might have been 12%; promises are made with incomplete information; and a tracker of targets risks becoming a Gosplan, where boxes are ticked and life is ignored. Two of these are gaps in the format: a card had no way to show what was expected without the promise, and no way to record that the world changed after it. A counterfactual offered after the fact ("without us it would have been 12%") cannot be checked and excuses any failure; a forecast published before the promise can be. Decided:
- `versions[n].parameters.baseline`: what an official or independent forecaster expected for the promise's measure, published on or before the day the version was recorded, with its value, unit, the date it is for, who made it, when, its source and an optional note on what it assumes. Never a party's figure or the site's own: the point is a yardstick neither side chose. In the target's unit when the version has one. A late field (FORMAT.md §9), so it can be added to published cards.
- A new engine event type, `circumstances_changed`: something outside the speaker's hands that changed what the promise could mean (a war, a pandemic, a price shock), dated, in neutral words, with its evidence. It never moves the status: the reader sees what changed and when, and judges.
- Cards show the baseline as "Forecast at the time" beside the target, with its source, in the page, the Markdown and six new open-data columns. The engine never scores a promise against it, and never says whether a change of circumstances excuses anything (principle 5).

The answer to the Gosplan worry stays what it was: the targets are the speakers' own words, the site sets none, and every figure has its source. Decided by Pavel Guzhikov on 22 October 2026 ("Draft decision 36: a baseline field (the independent forecast made at the time of the promise, with its source and date) and an 'overtaken by events' note. Branch and PR, no release yet.").
